Most ISVs think they have a Marketplace listing.

What they actually have is a URL.

There's a specific pattern I keep running into, and it's worth naming plainly: a listing goes live, gets screenshotted for a board deck or a partner-tier application, and then never gets touched again. It did its job as a checkbox. It has done nothing since.

That's not a small gap. It's a structural one, and it's quietly costing these companies co-sell deals they don't even know they're losing.

## Why This Keeps Happening

I don't think this is negligence. Most of these listings went up under real time pressure, built by someone who needed "AWS Marketplace presence" ticked off for a partner program requirement, a board update, or an AWS conversation, and who had a dozen other things competing for the same afternoon. Marketplace listing work is fiddly, it sits outside most people's core competency, and the guidance around it has shifted more than once. Nobody sat down and decided to build something that doesn't function. It just stopped being anyone's job the day it went live.

The problem is that AWS Marketplace isn't a static asset like a website. It's an active piece of co-sell infrastructure, and infrastructure that isn't maintained degrades in ways that aren't obvious from the outside.

## Why Co-Sell Actually Depends on This

Here's the mechanic that gets missed: in a healthy co-sell motion, the Marketplace listing is the call to action. When an AWS Account Manager wants to bring you into a deal, or a customer wants to move forward on their own initiative, the listing is where that intent is supposed to turn into motion. Request a private offer, get pricing tailored to the deal, execute.

But good co-sell isn't just one path working, it's flexibility. Not every AM or customer wants to engage the same way. Some want to go straight to a private offer and negotiate. Some want the customer trying the product first, which is exactly what demo requests and free trials are built for — they exist to give an AM something low-friction to point a curious customer at instead of a hard commercial ask. Some are dealing with a buyer who doesn't fit your default packaging at all, which is what tiering is for — it lets the same listing present a different shape of offer depending on who's actually looking at it. The point of all of it is the same: whatever engagement path a given deal actually needs, it should be available right there in the listing, not require a workaround, a phone call, or a separate process outside Marketplace entirely.

A listing that only offers one static option, or none, doesn't just fail at that one path. It fails at being the flexible front door co-sell needs it to be, and it pushes the AM back out into email and manual process for the parts of the deal Marketplace was supposed to handle.

If that path doesn't actually work, the whole motion stalls at the exact moment it should be accelerating.

And it's worse than a stall. Picture the AM's side of this. They've gone out on a limb, told a customer "just go to the Marketplace listing," maybe even flagged the opportunity internally because they're trying to help you close it. The customer clicks through and hits a wall of list pricing with no obvious way to engage, or no clear mechanism to request anything at all. Now the AM looks unprepared in front of their own customer, over something they had no way of predicting. That's not a neutral miss. That's the kind of experience that makes an AM quietly stop routing you deals.

## The Two Tells

There are two signals I see constantly that mark a listing as one of these checkbox exercises.

The first is no visible path to a private offer. AWS has moved the mechanics here more than once over the past few years, and a listing configured under old guidance often doesn't expose the ability to request one at all. Without it, there's no way for a customer or an AM to proactively drive a deal forward through the listing. It just sits there as a brochure.

The second is a wall of list pricing that's obviously not meant to be transacted at. A few years back, the standard advice was that you needed a public listing, but nobody wanted customers actually buying at that price, so the fix was to set list price absurdly high and handle everything real through private offers. That made sense at the time. What's changed is that public pricing isn't even a requirement anymore. So a listing still running that old workaround today doesn't read as "sophisticated seller protecting margin." It reads as amateur, and it actively confuses buyers who are trying to figure out whether they're looking at a real number.

Neither of these is a matter of the listing being sloppy or embarrassing. They're both just artifacts of guidance that was correct when it was set up and hasn't been revisited since.

## The Actual Cost

This is the part that doesn't show up on a dashboard anywhere: a listing built two or three years ago and never revisited is running on assumptions AWS has since moved past. Every update you don't absorb is a small tax on every deal that touches that listing, and it compounds. It's not one dramatic failure. It's a steady drip of friction, confused buyers, and AMs who quietly route around you next time, and none of it is visible until you go looking for it.

## Bottom Line

A Marketplace listing isn't a one-time deliverable, it's a live piece of co-sell infrastructure, and infrastructure that nobody owns drifts out of date. The fix isn't to build a better listing once. It's to treat "does this still reflect how AWS wants deals to move through Marketplace today" as a question worth asking on a regular cadence, not a question you answer once and file away. That's a big part of what a good third-party integrator brings to the table that's easy to overlook — they're not just building the listing, they're the ones staying on top of AWS's changes so your listing doesn't quietly fall behind them.
